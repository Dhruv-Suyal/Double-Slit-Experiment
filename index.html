01 — The setup

Three objects, one very strange screen

The apparatus in the 3D scene mirrors the real experiment first performed by Thomas Young in 1801, later repeated one particle at a time in the 20th century: a coherent light source, a barrier with two narrow slits, and a detection screen a fixed distance behind it.

  • Source — emits photons one at a time, all with the same wavelength (set by the slider), so every photon is coherent with itself.
  • Slit plate — an opaque barrier with two openings, whose separation (d) and width (a) you control directly.
  • Screen — a phosphor-coated detector. Every photon that arrives leaves a single, localized green dot — never a smear.

Run the simulation with the detector off and watch dots accumulate. Individually they look random. Given enough of them, they are not.

02 — Wave nature

Superposition and self-interference

When no measurement is made at the slits, light behaves exactly as Maxwell's equations predict: a wave. Each slit acts as a new source of circular (spherical, in 3D) wavefronts — this is Huygens' principle, visualized in the scene as the expanding cyan ripples. Where two wave crests meet, they add; where a crest meets a trough, they cancel.

The resulting light intensity striking the screen at horizontal position x is the classic double-slit diffraction formula:

I(x) = I₀ · [ sin(β)/β ]² · cos²(δ) β = (π·a·x) / (λ·L) — single-slit diffraction envelope δ = (π·d·x) / (λ·L) — two-slit interference terma = slit width · d = slit separation · λ = wavelength · L = slit-to-screen distance

The cos²(δ) term is the signature of interference — it is only possible because a single wave amplitude passed through both openings and recombined. Increase the wavelength and watch the bright fringes spread apart; widen the slit separation and watch them squeeze together. This is exactly the relationship the formula predicts.

💡

Try it: push the wavelength slider toward violet (short λ) and the fringes narrow. Push it toward red (long λ) and they widen. Fringe spacing scales as λL/d.

03 — Particle nature

Yet every landing is a single click

Here is the part that broke 19th-century physics: the screen never detects a smeared-out wave. It detects discrete, whole photons — one flash, one location, one energy quantum E = hf, every time. Light is quantized.

So which is it — wave or particle? The honest answer is that a single photon's wavefunction — a probability amplitude, not a physical ripple of stuff — spreads through both slits and interferes like a wave. But when it is detected, the wavefunction is sampled once, at one point, with a probability given by |ψ(x)|², exactly the intensity formula above.

This is why the simulation samples each photon's landing spot from that same I(x) distribution and draws it as a single point. Fire enough of them — hundreds, thousands — and the individually-random dots build, pixel by pixel, into the smooth interference fringes the wave equation predicted. No single photon "knows" the final picture; the pattern only exists statistically.

04 — The observer effect

Ask "which slit?" and the fringes vanish

Flip on the which-path detector in the panel above. Now every photon is tagged with which slit it passed through before it reaches the screen. Run it for a while, and the interference fringes disappear — replaced by two overlapping diffraction humps, one behind each slit, exactly what you'd expect from classical particles fired through two independent holes.

Nothing about the light's path changed. What changed is that path information now exists somewhere in the universe — in this simulation, in the detector; in a real lab, potentially in a polarizer, a scattered photon, or any physical system that becomes correlated with "which slit."

I_which-path(x) = [ sin(β₁)/β₁ ]² + [ sin(β₂)/β₂ ]² — the two amplitudes are added as PROBABILITIES, not as amplitudes. — the cross term cos²(δ) that produced fringes is gone.β₁, β₂ are each slit's own diffraction term, computed independently

This is the precise mathematical difference between the two regimes: interference requires summing complex amplitudes before squaring (|ψ₁+ψ₂|²); which-path information forces you to sum already-squared probabilities (|ψ₁|²+|ψ₂|²). The cross term that creates fringes only survives in the first case.

⚠️

Common myth: it is not "consciousness" or a human looking that causes this. Any physical interaction that records which-path information — a detector, a stray photon, even a poorly isolated measuring device — is enough to destroy the interference, whether or not anyone ever reads the result.

05 — Mechanism

Collapse, or just decoherence?

Historically this was described as the collapse of the wavefunction: before measurement, the photon exists in a superposition of "went through slit A" and "went through slit B"; the act of measurement forces it to "choose" one, instantaneously and irreversibly.

The modern, more mechanistic picture is decoherence. The photon never truly evolves in isolation — the moment it becomes entangled with a which-path detector, the combined system's state is:

|Ψ⟩ = |slit A⟩|detector saw A⟩ + |slit B⟩|detector saw B⟩

Because the detector states |saw A⟩ and |saw B⟩ are distinguishable, the cross-terms between them average out to zero for any observer who only looks at the photon — not because anything "collapsed," but because the interference information leaked into the environment and became practically unrecoverable. Both descriptions predict identical results; the simulation is agnostic between them, and mainstream physics still debates which is the more fundamental picture.

This is also the principle Niels Bohr called complementarity: wave-like and particle-like behaviour are two faces of the same system, but a single experiment can only ever reveal one face at a time. Precise which-path information and high-contrast interference are mutually exclusive — you can dial between them (try partial detector efficiency in real experiments), but you can never have perfect versions of both simultaneously.

06 — Why it matters

The one experiment that contains all of quantum weirdness

Richard Feynman used this exact setup in his lectures to argue that it holds the only real mystery of quantum mechanics — every other strange quantum phenomenon can be explained by reasoning through this one carefully. It has since been repeated with electrons, neutrons, and even large molecules like buckyballs, always with the same result: matter itself carries this wave-particle duality, not just light.

The double-slit experiment isn't a historical curiosity — it's the direct ancestor of modern quantum technologies. Quantum computing, quantum cryptography, and quantum sensing all exploit the same core fact this apparatus demonstrates: that measurement is not a passive act of observation, but a physical interaction that changes what is real to measure.
